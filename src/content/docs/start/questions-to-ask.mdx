A live-coding round usually ends with "Do you have any questions for me?" The interviewer is an engineer, often from the team you would join or one next to it, and three to five minutes are left. Two good questions turn the end of a technical test into a short conversation between colleagues.

<Aside type="tip" title="TL;DR">
- At the start, find out who is in front of you: their team and what they work on.
- At the end, ask two questions: one about their work, one about something that matters to you.
- A short follow-up on a good answer beats a third question.
- Leave salary, next steps and "How did I do?" for the recruiter.
</Aside>

## At the start: who is in front of you

Interviewers usually introduce themselves. If they do not, ask one short question before the task starts:

- "Before we start, which team are you on, and what do you work on?"
- "Could you tell me a bit about your role?"

Note the answer in one line. It decides which questions you ask at the end: an engineer from the team you would join can tell you about the daily work; an engineer from another team can tell you how teams work together.

## At the end: two questions

There is usually time for two questions. Use a simple rule: **one about them, one about what matters to you**. If an answer is long and interesting, ask one follow-up on it rather than a third question.

A bridge to your own experience can help, but keep it to one sentence, not a story: "In my last team I built a reconciliation job, so I am curious how you do it."

### About their work

Good when the interviewer works on, or close to, the product you would work on:

1. "Is today's problem close to something you work on? How does it look in your real system?"
   Follow-up: "And what happens if the same event arrives twice?"
2. "How do you make sure the numbers are right? What catches a mistake before a customer sees it?"
3. "What would you want from a new person on this team in the first months?"
   Follow-up: "What is one thing that slows you down today?"

Good when the interviewer is from another team:

1. "How does your team work with other teams, for example when you need a change in their code?"
2. "What do teams here share, like on-call, code review or common libraries, and what does each team decide on its own?"
3. "When a problem crosses two teams' systems, how do you find and fix it together?"

### About what matters to you

Pick the one you really need to know to decide whether you want the job:

- **Remote work:** "What helps you most to work well across time zones, and what is still hard?"
- **Balance in practice:** "How does the workload look during an incident or a big launch?"
- **What keeps people:** "What do you enjoy most about working here, and what is the hardest part?"
- **Closeness to users:** "How close are engineers to the customers? Do you talk to them directly?"
- **AI at work:** "How does your team use AI in daily work, and where do you draw the line?"
- **Decisions:** "How are bigger technical decisions made here: a written proposal, a design review, or inside the team?"
- **Growth:** "What does a good year look like for an engineer here? How do people grow?"

## Closing

Thank them, briefly and warmly, and stop:

- "Thank you for your time. I enjoyed working on this problem with you."

## What not to ask in this round

Some questions are fine in the process, but not in a technical round with an engineer:

- **Salary, benefits, relocation or contract details.** These are for the recruiter.
- **Next steps and timelines.** The recruiter will tell you; from an engineer it sounds awkward.
- **"How did I do?"** Many companies do not give feedback during the interview, and the question puts the engineer in an uncomfortable position.
- **What is written on the company's website.** It shows that you did not read it.
- **Company finances or strategy.** An engineer may not know, and the conversation becomes awkward. Ask a founder, a manager or a director in their round.

<Aside type="note" title="In the interview">
Build your first question from the task you just solved: "Today's task had duplicate webhooks. How do you deal with them in your real system?" It shows that you listened, and the interviewer gets to talk about their own work. Prepare four or five questions at home, then pick two in the moment.
</Aside>
